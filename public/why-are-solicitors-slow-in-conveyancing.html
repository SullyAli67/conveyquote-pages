The honest answer

Most conveyancing delay is structural – but not all of it

If you are eight weeks into a purchase and nothing appears to have happened, the assumption is usually that your solicitor is sitting on the file. Sometimes that is exactly what is happening. Far more often, the file is moving but is waiting on somebody else entirely – a council search department, a managing agent, a mortgage lender, or the solicitor at the other end of the chain.

Conveyancing is not one long task. It is a sequence of roughly forty small tasks, and a large proportion of them cannot start until a third party has responded. Your solicitor has no power to compel any of those third parties to move faster. When people describe conveyancing as slow, what they are usually describing is the compound effect of many short waits stacked end to end, with nobody explaining which one is currently in play.

That said, the profession is not blameless. High-volume firms running eighty or more live files per fee earner genuinely do drop things, and poor communication turns a normal delay into an infuriating one. The useful skill is being able to tell the two apart – because the response is completely different. This page sets out both.

The real causes

Seven things that actually hold up your transaction

These are the genuine bottlenecks, in rough order of how often they are the culprit. Note how few of them are within your solicitor's control – and that the ones which are tend to be about chasing, not about legal work.

01

Local authority search backlogs

Every purchase needs a local authority search covering planning history, building regulations, road adoption and enforcement notices. Turnaround is set entirely by the council and varies enormously – some return in days, others take six to eight weeks at peak. Your solicitor orders it on day one and then waits like everybody else.

Outside solicitor's control
02

Leasehold management packs

If you are buying a flat, the freeholder or managing agent must supply a pack containing service charge accounts, ground rent details, insurance, major works schedules and fire safety information. There is no statutory deadline for producing it. Four to eight weeks is common, and the pack usually costs £200–£400.

Outside solicitor's control
03

Chain dependencies

In a chain, exchange happens simultaneously for everyone or not at all. A buyer four properties above you whose mortgage offer is delayed will hold up your completion, no matter how organised you and your solicitor are. Longer chains do not just add risk – they add the waiting time of every link.

Outside solicitor's control
04

Mortgage lender processing

Your formal mortgage offer has to be issued, the lender's own conditions satisfied, and funds requested and released ahead of completion. Underwriting queries, a down-valuation, or a change in your circumstances can each add weeks. Lenders also impose their own deadlines for drawing down funds.

Outside solicitor's control
05

Enquiries going back and forth

Your solicitor raises enquiries; the seller's solicitor must ask the seller; the seller finds the paperwork; the answer comes back; it prompts a follow-up. Each round trip can take a fortnight, and a title defect or missing building regulations certificate can generate several. This is where a proactive firm earns its fee.

Partly controllable
06

ID, AML and source of funds

Solicitors are legally required to verify your identity and establish where your money came from. Gifted deposits need a letter and evidence from the giver. Funds held abroad, held in crypto, or recently moved between accounts take substantially longer to evidence. Delays here are almost always waiting on documents from the client.

Usually waiting on you
07

Land Registry processing

Registration happens after you have moved in, so it rarely delays completion – but it matters if you are selling a property bought recently, or dealing with a lease extension or title split. Registry backlogs on complex applications have run to many months, and a pending application can complicate an onward sale.

Outside solicitor's control
Being straight about it

When it genuinely is the firm

Everything above is a real, structural cause of delay. But it would be dishonest to pretend that every slow transaction has an innocent explanation. Some firms are the bottleneck, and there are recognisable signs. Here is what to look for.

  • Caseloads that make attention impossible Some volume conveyancing operations run eighty to a hundred live files per fee earner. At that level, files are worked reactively – whoever shouts loudest gets attention that week, and quiet files simply sit. A firm handling a sensible caseload can chase third parties before they become a problem rather than after.
  • No named contact who knows your matter If every call reaches a different person who has to read the file from scratch, nobody owns your transaction. Case-tracking portals are useful, but they are not a substitute for someone who can tell you in one sentence what the file is waiting on.
  • Reactive rather than proactive chasing The difference between a twelve-week and an eighteen-week transaction is usually whether somebody chased the managing agent at week two or waited until you asked at week six. Nothing about that is legal skill – it is diary management, and it is entirely within the firm's control.
  • A quote that quietly grows Fees that appear late in the process – for a leasehold supplement, a gifted deposit, an expedited transfer, a file closure charge – are a sign the original quote was written to win the instruction rather than to describe the work. It is not a delay in itself, but it correlates strongly with how a firm treats its clients generally.
  • Slow to answer the other side Your solicitor cannot make the seller's solicitor respond quickly. But they can respond quickly themselves. If the other side is repeatedly waiting on your firm, you are the reason the chain is slow – and you will usually only discover this by asking your estate agent, who talks to both sides.

Diagnose it

How to tell which one you are dealing with

Ask your solicitor one specific question: "What is the file currently waiting on, who is it waiting on, and when were they last chased?" A firm that is on top of your matter will answer that in a sentence. Use the answer against this table.

What you are seeing Most likely cause What to do about it
Weeks of silence early on Searches are out and have not returned. This is the single most common "nothing is happening" phase, and genuinely nothing can happen until they land. Ask for the date searches were ordered and the council's current published turnaround. If they were not ordered in the first week, that is a legitimate complaint.
Silence on a flat purchase The management pack has been requested and the managing agent has not supplied it. Four to eight weeks is normal and your solicitor cannot compel it. Ask the seller directly, through the estate agent, to chase their managing agent. Sellers often get a faster response than solicitors do.
Everything ready, no exchange date You are waiting on the chain. Exchange is simultaneous, so the slowest party sets the date for everyone. Ask your estate agent where the holdup sits in the chain. Agents have visibility across all parties that your solicitor does not.
Repeated requests for the same documents Either the file is being handled by different people each time, or documents supplied have not been logged properly. Send everything again in one email, ask for written confirmation of receipt, and ask who your named contact is.
Estate agent says your side is holding it up Your firm is slow to respond to the other side's solicitor. The agent talks to both parties and is usually telling the truth. Escalate in writing to the supervising partner, quoting specific dates. This is the situation where escalation genuinely works.
Nobody can say what the file is waiting on The file is not being actively managed. This is the clearest single indicator of a firm-side problem. Escalate to the supervising partner, then to the firm's formal complaints procedure. All SRA-regulated firms must have one.
Take control

Five things that genuinely speed your transaction up

You have more influence than you think – but only over specific parts of the process. These are the ones that measurably shorten a transaction.

1

Instruct before your offer is accepted

You can instruct a solicitor and complete identity checks as soon as you are seriously looking. Doing so removes one to two weeks of dead time at the very start, when the file would otherwise be waiting on your paperwork rather than on any legal work.

2

Return ID and source-of-funds evidence immediately

Nothing substantive starts until anti-money-laundering checks clear. If part of your deposit is a gift, get the letter and the giver's bank statements early – this is one of the most common self-inflicted delays and it is entirely avoidable.

3

If selling, complete your property forms in week one

The TA6 and TA10 forms, plus guarantees, building regulations certificates and warranties, are what the buyer's solicitor raises enquiries against. Complete and thorough forms at the outset prevent whole rounds of enquiries later.

4

Chase your own lender directly

Your solicitor cannot escalate within your bank – you can. If your mortgage offer is the holdup, calling your lender or broker yourself is consistently faster than waiting for the offer to arrive through the normal channel.

5

Ask one specific question a week, in writing

A weekly email asking what the file is waiting on, who it is waiting on, and when they were last chased is more effective than daily phone calls. It creates a written record, takes seconds to answer, and makes a neglected file visible.

Common questions

Frequently asked questions about conveyancing delays

How long should conveyancing actually take?

A straightforward freehold purchase or sale usually completes within ten to fourteen weeks of an offer being accepted. Leasehold transactions typically take sixteen weeks or more because of the management pack and the additional lease review. A remortgage is usually four to six weeks. New build purchases run to the developer's timetable, which can mean either a very short exchange deadline or a long wait for practical completion. If you are inside these ranges, your transaction is progressing normally even if it does not feel like it.

Can I chase my solicitor, and how often?

Yes, and you should. A weekly check-in is reasonable and most firms expect it. Ask specific questions rather than general ones: instead of asking whether there is any news, ask what the file is currently waiting on, who it is waiting on, and when that party was last chased. Daily calls are counterproductive because time spent answering the phone is time not spent progressing your file, but a weekly written update request is entirely fair and creates a record.

Can I change solicitor mid-transaction?

You can, but it is rarely the right answer. Changing firm means your new solicitor starts the file again, repeats identity and source-of-funds checks, and often re-raises enquiries – typically adding four to six weeks and a second set of fees. You will usually pay the first firm for work already done. Switching only makes sense where a firm has genuinely stopped working the file and is unresponsive to formal complaints. In most cases, escalating to the supervising partner is faster and cheaper.

Does paying more for a solicitor make conveyancing faster?

Not directly. A higher fee does not speed up a local authority search, a management company, or a mortgage lender – and those are where most delay actually sits. What a better firm buys you is responsiveness: fewer files per fee earner, a named contact who knows your matter, and proactive chasing of third parties rather than waiting for them. That often shortens a transaction by a few weeks, but it does so through attention, not through paying a premium for the same process.

Why is leasehold conveyancing so much slower than freehold?

Leasehold requires a management pack from the freeholder or managing agent, containing service charge accounts, ground rent details, building insurance, major works schedules and fire safety information. These packs are produced by third parties who are under no statutory deadline, commonly cost between two and four hundred pounds, and routinely take four to eight weeks to arrive. Your solicitor cannot compel their release. On top of that, the lease itself must be reviewed in detail and any defects raised with the freeholder, which can add several further weeks.
